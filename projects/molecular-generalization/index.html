Testing whether molecular models actually generalize

A case study in representation, chemistry-aware validation, and honest comparison between classical baselines and pretrained molecular embeddings.

A central question in molecular ML is not simply which model scores best on a benchmark, but which model actually generalizes when the chemistry shifts.

Figure 1. The benchmark was designed around a chemistry-aware notion of generalization rather than a random split.

The scientific problem

Molecular ML results can look strong when train and test molecules are highly similar. I wanted to build a more realistic benchmark that asked whether a model could extrapolate to structurally less familiar compounds. That required thinking carefully about the split policy, not only the model family.

Approach

I used similarity-based grouping to define train/test partitions, then compared classical descriptor-based models against pretrained molecular embeddings. The benchmark emphasized reproducibility and fair comparison rather than chasing a single headline number.

Figure 2. Under the similarity-aware evaluation used here, a descriptor-based XGBoost model outperformed a pretrained-embedding baseline on the critical-temperature benchmark.

Key result

One of the clearest outcomes was conceptual rather than purely numerical: model sophistication is not the same as robust generalization. In this benchmark, the classical descriptor baseline remained stronger under the selected test of structural novelty.

Why this matters

This work reinforced an idea that cuts across many scientific ML settings: evaluation design determines what a benchmark really measures. If the question is generalization under chemical distribution shift, then the split policy must reflect that question.
