Industry · Battery R&D · Lab-in-the-loop

Closed-loop design of battery electrolytes

Predicting mixture properties, optimizing under constraints, and letting laboratory evidence reshape the next search.

Question

Can we propose new electrolyte formulations, test them in the lab, and use what the lab finds to improve the next round?

Approach

Mixture-aware GNN surrogates (DiffMix) for conductivity, viscosity, and flash point, with constrained multi-objective optimization (NSGA-III).

Result

Diverse candidates went to lab testing. Lab results led to revised composition bounds and a flagged unreliable viscosity regime.

My role

Built the training data, trained and validated the surrogates, connected them to the optimizer, and worked with experimental collaborators on the feedback.

The problem

An electrolyte is a mixture of solvents, salts, and additives. Its properties depend on which components are present, how much of each, and how they interact. We wanted three things at once: higher ionic conductivity, lower viscosity, and a higher flash point for safety. These objectives pull against each other, and every candidate also had to use allowed components, stay within composition limits, and be something the lab could actually prepare.

Predicting mixture properties

Single-molecule models are not enough here: the same molecules at different fractions behave differently. I trained mixture-aware graph neural network surrogates for viscosity, ionic conductivity, and flash point, based on the published DiffMix architecture (Zhu et al., Nature Communications, 2024). My work was building the training data from the literature, training and validating the models, and connecting them to the optimizer. Validation held out whole component combinations, so the models were tested on mixtures whose component sets they had not seen.

Searching for trade-offs, not one optimum

The optimizer generated candidate compositions, scored them with the surrogates, checked feasibility, kept the non-dominated set while preserving diversity, and evolved the next generation. The result was a set of formulations spanning the trade-offs: some safety-leaning, some conductivity-leaning, some balanced. From these we shortlisted diverse candidates for laboratory testing.

When the lab changed the problem

Two results from the lab changed the computational workflow itself.

Feasibility. Some proposed formulations would not mix during preparation. The property predictions were not wrong as such: miscibility was simply not part of the problem the optimizer had been given. We confirmed the failures, located the composition region where they occurred, and revised the optimizer's composition bounds so the next search stayed in the region that can actually be made.

Model reliability. For a group of measured formulations, viscosity was systematically overpredicted, with every error in the same direction. After repeat measurements and calibration checks ruled out the instrument, the poorly predicted formulations turned out to share a concentrated-electrolyte regime that was underrepresented in the training data. We flagged predictions in that regime as unreliable for candidate prioritization and targeted it for new training data.

Limitations

  • Miscibility was not modeled; it is handled through constraints learned from lab failures, so new chemistries can still fail in new ways.
  • Surrogate reliability varies by regime; predictions in the flagged concentrated regime need more training data before they can drive selection.

What I took from it

  • A surrogate that is accurate on average can still fail systematically in exactly the regions an optimizer finds most interesting.
  • Experimental feedback can change the constraints and the trust placed in a model, not only its training data.
  • A model and an optimizer help only when the objectives and constraints reflect physical reality, and the lab is how you find out whether they do.
